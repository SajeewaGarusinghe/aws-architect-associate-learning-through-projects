Every other lab used IAM as scenery. This one makes it the subject. Nothing is deployed that runs, stores or serves anything — the entire lab is roles, policies and the policy simulator, which means it costs nothing at all and can be left running as long as you like.

Scope In scope: the evaluation order AWS actually applies, permission boundaries, explicit versus implicit deny, identity versus resource policies, ABAC with tag conditions, and the policy simulator. Out of scope: Organizations and service control policies, identity federation, and IAM Identity Center — all worth knowing, none of which a single account can demonstrate.

Reference architecture

01. Deny wins, then boundaries, then allows
In one line

A policy that says Allow does not mean access is granted — the request is granted only if no explicit deny matches, every boundary in force permits it, and at least one allow exists; and the two roles in this lab prove it by carrying the same permission policy to opposite outcomes.

Context & requirements

What it must do

  • Show a boundary shrinking an over-broad policy
  • Show an explicit deny beating an allow
  • Show a resource policy granting on its own
  • Make each outcome checkable, not asserted

How it must behave

  • No IAM users, no access keys, roles only
  • Every claim verifiable with the simulator
  • A control case for every demonstration
  • Nothing that can actually be exploited

Constraints

  • No compute, no network, no data
  • Cost exactly zero
  • Single account — no Organizations
  • Destroyed by a single delete-stack
High-level design
The boundary policy
Allows only s3:Get* and s3:List*. Why: a permission boundary grants nothing. It sets a ceiling, and effective permissions become the intersection of the identity policy and the boundary.
The over-broad policy
Allows s3:* and ec2:* on *. Why: deliberately alarming, and attached identically to both roles. Any difference in what they can do comes from something other than this policy.
Bounded role
The over-broad policy plus the boundary. Why: effective permissions collapse to S3 reads. Every EC2 action and every S3 write is refused, even though the attached policy plainly allows them.
Unbounded role
The same over-broad policy, no boundary. Why: the control case. Comparing the two in the simulator isolates the boundary as the only variable — which is what makes this a demonstration rather than a claim.
ABAC role
May start and stop instances only where aws:ResourceTag/team equals its own aws:PrincipalTag/team. Why: one policy that works for any number of teams. Attribute-based access control is how you stop writing a policy per team, and it scales where role-per-team does not.
The vault bucket
Carries a resource policy with two statements. Why: it is the stage for the other two lessons — one statement denies a role by name, the other grants a role that has no S3 permissions of its own.
The policy simulator
iam simulate-principal-policy, called read-only. Why: it evaluates a request exactly as IAM would, without performing it, and returns allowed, implicitDeny or explicitDeny — the three outcomes this whole lab is about.

Data flow — how a request is evaluated
1
A principal makes a request. AWS gathers every policy that applies: identity policies, resource policies, permission boundaries, session policies, and service control policies if the account is in an Organization.
2
Explicit deny wins immediately. If any policy contains a matching Deny, evaluation stops and the request fails. Nothing overrides this — not an administrator policy, not the account root.
3
Service control policies are checked next. An SCP that does not allow the action stops the request even for the root user of that account.
4
Permission boundaries are checked. If a boundary is attached and does not allow the action, the request is denied — this is where the bounded role loses every EC2 action.
5
Identity and resource policies are evaluated. Within the same account, an allow in either is sufficient — which is why the ABAC role can read the vault with no S3 permission of its own.
6
If nothing allowed the action, the result is implicit deny: the default. This is the difference the simulator reports between implicitDeny (nobody granted it) and explicitDeny (somebody forbade it).
Key decisions
How do you let a team create roles without letting them escalate privilege?
Give them iam:* and trust them
Anyone who can create a role and attach a policy can grant themselves administrator. This is privilege escalation by design, and it is the classic finding in an access review.
Approve every role by hand
Safe and unscalable. It makes the security team a queue, and teams route around queues.
Require a permission boundary on roles they create
Chosen. A condition requiring iam:PermissionsBoundary on CreateRole means every role they make is capped, whatever policy they attach. This is the canonical use of boundaries and a frequent exam scenario.
How do you grant access per team as the number of teams grows?
One policy and one role per team
Role-based access control. Correct, and it multiplies: twenty teams means twenty policies to write, review and keep consistent as resources change.
One policy with tag conditions
Chosen. aws:ResourceTag/team matched against aws:PrincipalTag/team is a single policy that works for every team, now and for teams that do not exist yet. The cost is that tagging discipline becomes a security control.
The order, in one line Explicit deny → SCPs → permission boundaries → identity and resource policies → implicit deny. Almost every IAM exam question is answerable by walking this list. "Why can't this role do X when the policy allows it?" is a boundary or an explicit deny. "Why can this role do X when its policy never mentions it?" is a resource policy.
Security · scaling · reliability · cost
Data protection
No IAM users and no access keys exist anywhere in this curriculum — every principal is a role assumed for temporary credentials. The bucket blocks all public access, and the only data it will ever hold is a test file you put there.
Scaling
The relevant limits are quotas: 5,000 roles per account, 10 managed policies per role, 6,144 characters per managed policy. Policy size is the one teams actually hit, and it is why broad resource patterns and ABAC beat long literal lists.
Failure modes
IAM's failure mode is rarely an outage — it is a policy that grants more than intended. Changes are eventually consistent, so a fresh policy may take seconds to apply everywhere, which makes "I fixed it but it still fails" a real and confusing experience.
Cost levers
None. IAM roles, policies, the simulator, and Access Analyzer's external-access findings are all free. This lab is the one place in the curriculum where leaving the stack running costs nothing whatsoever.
Operational notes~20 min, $0
Deploy
Under a minute. IAM resources are records, not machines. Note that CloudFormation requires CAPABILITY_NAMED_IAM here because the roles have explicit names.
Observe
Run the simulator against both roles for the same action. Identical policies, opposite answers — that gap is the boundary, and seeing it stated as implicitDeny versus allowed makes it concrete.
Break
Attempt s3:GetObject on the vault as the unbounded role and get explicitDeny. Then attach an administrator policy to that role in the console and try again — it is still denied, which is the entire point of explicit deny.
Destroy
Under a minute. The bucket must be empty first if you uploaded a test object.
Watch these three things
  1. The exact words implicitDeny and explicitDeny. They are different failures with different fixes, and the simulator is the only place you see the distinction plainly.
  2. That the bounded role loses s3:PutObject too, not just EC2. The boundary allows s3:Get* and s3:List* — a write was never inside it.
  3. The ABAC role reading the vault with no S3 statement in its own policy. That access comes entirely from the bucket's resource policy.
Red flag — don't Never use a permission boundary as a way to grant permissions. A boundary alone gives a principal nothing at all — attach a boundary with no identity policy and the role can do nothing. Boundaries only ever subtract. The mirror-image mistake is assuming a boundary protects a role that has no boundary attached: it applies to the principal that carries it, and to nothing else.

Deliberately absent. No service control policies — they need AWS Organizations, and a single account cannot show them. Worth knowing regardless: an SCP is another ceiling, applied to an entire account, and like a boundary it grants nothing. No federation or IAM Identity Center, which is how real organisations avoid IAM users entirely. No cross-account role assumption, though the mechanism is exactly the trust policy already on these roles, pointed at a different account number.

The two questions to ask of any IAM problem First: is something explicitly denying it? Check resource policies, boundaries and SCPs, not just the identity policy — an explicit deny anywhere ends the discussion. Second: is anything actually allowing it? An action nobody granted fails by default, and an empty result from the simulator means implicit deny, not a broken simulator. Nearly every real IAM incident is one of these two, and the simulator answers both in seconds.
